# 1 · Why a non-deterministic system cannot be shipped on judgement alone

*Lecture 37, part 1 of 8 · ~15 min reading*

## 1.1 The system you are shipping is not the system you looked at

Every engineer who has shipped an LLM feature has done this: change the prompt, run five or ten inputs through it, read the outputs, nod, merge. The course has spent weeks 17–18 building an agent that calls tools in a loop, recovers from failures, and resists injection. This week asks the uncomfortable question: how do you *know* any of that works — and the answer "I ran it and looked" is not an answer, for a reason that is structural rather than a matter of diligence.

The reason is that the thing you looked at is one draw from a distribution, and the thing you shipped is the distribution. Two facts make this bite.

**First, the model is non-deterministic even when you ask it not to be.** Thinking Machines Lab's investigation "Defeating Nondeterminism in LLM Inference" (September 2025) is blunt: "Even when we adjust the temperature down to 0 (thus making the sampling theoretically deterministic), LLM APIs are still not deterministic in practice." The folk explanation — GPUs, floating point, concurrency — is, in their words, "not entirely wrong, [but] it doesn't reveal the full picture." Running "the same matrix multiplication on the same data repeatedly will always provide bitwise equal results." The real cause is that "the load (and thus batch-size) nondeterministically varies": your request's numerics depend on which *other* requests happen to share its batch on the server, i.e. on traffic you neither control nor observe. In their experiment, one prompt sampled 1,000 times at temperature 0 produced "80 unique completions, with the most common occurring 78 times," and "the first instance of diverging completions occurs at the 103rd token." That is a long agreement prefix followed by divergence — exactly the shape that makes a human reviewer confident and wrong.

The vendors say the same thing in their own contracts. OpenAI's API guide: the APIs are "non-deterministic by default"; with a `seed`, "our system will make a best effort to sample deterministically," and the `system_fingerprint` field exists because "if this value is different, you may see different outputs due to changes we've made on our systems." Their cookbook measured it: average inter-run output distance fell from 0.114 without a seed to 0.045 with `seed=123` and temperature 0. It fell. It did not reach zero.

**Second, the substrate changes under you.** The fingerprint is an admission that the backend you certified on Monday may not be the backend serving on Thursday. Anthropic's model-versioning page makes the opposite promise — "Anthropic does not update the weights or configuration of an existing model ID. When an updated version is available, it ships under a new model ID" — which is exactly why pinning a model ID is the first line of every eval config (part 8). But your retrieval index changes, your tool schemas change, your prompt changes, and the traffic changes. A one-time inspection certifies a point; you are operating a process.

## 1.2 The sample-size argument

Suppose the system is good. Suppose its residual defect rate is what Eugene Yan reports as typical for grounded generation: "The typical factual inconsistency/irrelevance rate is 5–10 %, even after grounding via RAG and good prompt engineering." Now suppose you eyeball ten outputs. At a 5 % defect rate the probability that all ten look fine is `0.95^10 ≈ 0.60`. Six times out of ten your vibe check passes a system that will produce a defect in one of every twenty responses — at 100,000 responses a day, 5,000 defects a day. At a 10 % rate, `0.9^10 ≈ 0.35`: you still wave it through a third of the time.

Turn it around. To have a 95 % chance of seeing *at least one* defect at a 5 % rate you need `n` with `0.95^n ≤ 0.05`, i.e. `n ≥ 59` samples. And seeing one defect tells you almost nothing about the *rate*: part 4 does the arithmetic on confidence intervals, but the headline is that at `n = 40` a measured 80 % pass rate has a 95 % interval of roughly ±12 percentage points. Judgement on a handful of examples cannot distinguish a 95 % system from an 85 % one, and that ten-point gap is the difference between a product and a support-ticket generator.

Hamel Husain's case study of Lucy, a real-estate assistant, names the failure exactly: the team stalled because of "limited visibility into the AI system's effectiveness across tasks beyond vibe checks." The fix was not a better model. It was hundreds of unit tests, traces you can read, and a human-agreement check on the judge — the machinery this lecture and the next build.

## 1.3 Why this is different from ordinary software

You already test software. The difference is where the uncertainty lives. In a deterministic service the uncertainty is in *your understanding* of the code; a passing test on one run means a passing test on every run with the same inputs, so `n = 1` is enough per case. In an LLM system the uncertainty is in *the component itself*. Google's own testing blog reports that "almost 16 % of our tests have some level of flakiness" and that "about 84 % of the transitions we observe from pass to fail involve a flaky test" — and their remedy was statistical: a flaky-marked test "report[s] a failure only if it fails 3 times in a row," and persistent offenders are quarantined. Ordinary software treats non-determinism as a bug to be engineered out. An LLM system has non-determinism as a *property*, so the machinery Google reached for reluctantly — repetition, thresholds, quarantine — is the machinery you need by design.

Anthropic's engineering guide on agent evals makes the reporting consequence explicit: "a task that passed on one eval run might fail on the next," so you report `pass@k` ("the likelihood that an agent gets at least one correct solution in k attempts") or `pass^k` ("the probability that all k trials succeed"), not a single verdict. τ-bench, which introduced the `pass^k` metric, found that "even state-of-the-art function calling agents (like gpt-4o) succeed on <50 % of the tasks" and were "quite inconsistent (pass^8 <25 % in retail)": a check that passes once and fails seven times out of eight is a check you would have shipped on judgement.

## 1.4 What judgement is still for

None of this says human judgement is worthless. It says judgement is an *input to the measurement*, not a substitute for it. Part 7 argues that the highest-leverage activity in the whole discipline — reading real traces and naming what went wrong — is pure judgement. Shreya Shankar and colleagues showed why it cannot be skipped: "users need criteria to grade outputs, but grading outputs helps users define criteria." Judgement produces the criteria; the harness applies them at a sample size judgement cannot reach; Lecture 38 calibrates a model to apply the subjective ones. The order matters. Look first, to decide *what* to measure; then measure, because looking cannot tell you *how often*.

## 1.5 What to carry forward

The output you inspected is one sample from a distribution that depends on server load you cannot see, and the substrate producing it changes without notice. At a realistic 5–10 % defect rate a ten-example review passes a bad system most of the time, and no review of any size distinguishes a 95 % system from an 85 % one without counting. Every judgement you make about quality therefore has to become a *case* — an input, a check, an expected outcome — that runs many times, on every change, against a version-pinned system, and reports a rate with an interval. The rest of this lecture is how to build the deterministic half of that.
