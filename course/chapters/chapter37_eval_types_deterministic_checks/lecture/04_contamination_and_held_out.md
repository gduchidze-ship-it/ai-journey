# 4 · Contaminating your own eval set — and keeping a held-out slice you look at rarely

*Lecture 37, part 4 of 8 · ~25 min reading*

## 4.1 The loop that turns a test set into a training set

Here is what prompt tuning looks like from the outside. You have a case set. You change the prompt. You run the set. The score went up: keep the change. The score went down: revert. Repeat forty times over a month.

Now describe that loop in the vocabulary of part 1 of the course. You have a hypothesis space (prompts), a training signal (the score on the case set), and a selection rule (keep what scores higher). That is *fitting*. The parameters are words instead of weights and the optimizer is you instead of Adam, but the case set is playing the role of training data, and the number it reports at the end is a *training* score. It will be higher than the score on cases you have never seen, for the same reason a training loss is lower than a validation loss — and you have no validation loss, because you spent the only set you had on the fitting.

No source says "forty rounds turns it into a training set" in those words. The mechanism, however, is one of the best-established results in learning theory, and it was worked out for exactly this situation.

## 4.2 The evidence: leaderboards and reusable holdouts

**Blum & Hardt (2015)** studied Kaggle, where competitors submit predictions and see a score on a held-out public leaderboard. "As participants are allowed to repeatedly evaluate their submissions on the leaderboard, they may begin to overfit to the holdout data that supports the leaderboard." Kaggle's own forums "report on the problem of 'overfitting to the holdout' meaning that some scores on the public leaderboard are inflated." The mechanism is dependence: "Participants may begin to incorporate the feedback from the leaderboard into the design of their classifier thus creating a dependence between the classifier" and the holdout, after which "it is well known that the holdout set no longer gives an unbiased estimate of the classifier's true performance." Replace *participant* with *you*, *classifier* with *prompt*, *leaderboard* with *your eval run*, and the sentence is about this week. Their dataset was one Kaggle competition with 12,000 test rows and 1,785 submissions from 200 teams — roughly nine looks per team.

Their fix, the *Ladder*, is a policy you can adopt tomorrow: only report a new score when it beats the incumbent by more than a step size `η`, and round it. In their notation: if `R_S(f_t) < R_{t−1} − η` then update, else keep the old number. Translated: **do not accept a prompt change on the strength of a one-case improvement**, and do not read the third decimal. Part 8 turns this into the gate's threshold.

**Dwork, Feldman, Hardt, Pitassi, Reingold & Roth (*Science*, 2015)** priced the damage. "Adapting the analysis to data results in an implicit multiple comparisons problem that is not captured in the reported significance levels." Their demonstration: 10,000 samples in 10,000 dimensions with *random* labels, so that "no classifier should exceed 50 % accuracy." An analyst who selects features by looking at the holdout and reports on the same holdout gets "reported accuracy of > 63 ± 0.4 % for k = 500." A coin flip, measured as 63 %, by reusing one holdout 500 times. Their remedy, *Thresholdout*, gives "the analyst … unfettered access to the training data set but can only access the holdout set via an algorithm" that adds noise and only answers when train and holdout disagree by more than a threshold — a formalization of "look at the held-out slice rarely, and only to confirm."

**Recht, Roelofs, Schmidt & Shankar (2019)** rebuilt the CIFAR-10 and ImageNet test sets from the original recipe and found "accuracy drops of 3 % – 15 % on CIFAR-10 and 11 % – 14 % on ImageNet." Read the paper carefully before you quote it, because its own conclusion is the nuance you need: "our results suggest that the accuracy drops are not caused by adaptivity, but by the models' inability to generalize to slightly 'harder' images than those found in the original test sets." Two different things break a reused eval set: **adaptive overfitting** (Blum–Hardt, Dwork) and **the set drifting away from the real distribution** (Recht). They need different fixes — a held-out slice for the first, a refresh policy for the second — and part 4.5 gives both.

**LLM benchmarks show the endgame.** Scale AI's GSM1k rebuilt GSM8k from the same recipe and "observe[d] accuracy drops of up to 8 %, with several families of models showing evidence of systematic overfitting," with a Spearman `r² = 0.36` between a model's probability of *generating* a GSM8k example and its GSM8k-to-GSM1k gap — "suggesting that some models may have partially memorized GSM8k." OpenAI stopped reporting SWE-bench Verified in February 2026 because "all frontier models tested were able to reproduce the original human-written bug fix, indicating they encountered problems during training," alongside material issues in 59.4 % of audited items — 35.5 % of them tests that "enforce specific implementation details, invalidating many functionally correct submissions." Sainz et al. name the worst case: "when a Large Language Model is trained on the test split of a benchmark, and then evaluated in the same benchmark," with the consequence that "wrong scientific conclusions [are] published while other correct ones are discarded."

You are not training a model on your forty cases. But you *are* selecting among prompts on them, and if you ever fine-tune (Lecture 20) or add few-shot examples to a prompt, the cases you used become literally training data. Hamel's rule for judges applies to the system too: "Never place dev or test examples in the judge prompt."

## 4.3 The steel-man: prompts overfit slowly

Be honest about the strength of the claim. Akinwande, Jiang, Sam & Kolter (2023) argue that discrete prompts are a small hypothesis class — a prompt is a short string chosen from a language model's prior — and derive PAC-Bayes bounds that are "remarkably tight"; empirically, "when a prompt is manually engineered to achieve low error on a given training set … the approach still performs well on held-out test data." Prompt tuning overfits *less per step* than gradient descent, because each step can only move the system a little and the moves are constrained to natural language.

Less per step is not never. The Dwork result does not care what the hypothesis class is; it counts *selections*. Forty accepted revisions on a forty-case set is one selection per case. The honest position: your eval score after a month of tuning is optimistically biased by an amount that grows with the number of changes you accepted because the score went up, and you cannot know the size of the bias from the set you tuned on. Which is why you keep another one.

## 4.4 The held-out slice

The discipline has three parts: a slice you do not tune on, a rule for how often you look, and a size that makes the look worth anything.

**The split.** Both Hamel's judge guide and the OpenAI cookbook he co-authored prescribe the same shape: a *train* slice of 10–20 % whose "only job is to provide the 'few-shot' examples," a *dev* (or validation) slice of ~40 % "where you will iteratively improve," and a *test* slice of ~40 % that "is your report card. After tuning, run the judge on this set **one time**." That sentence is the strongest first-party statement of the one-look rule in either major lab's documentation. For the deterministic harness this week, the same structure applies with different names: a **working set** you run on every change and use to decide what to fix, and a **held-out slice** you run on a schedule and never use to choose between two prompts.

**The rule.** Look at the held-out slice when you are about to *ship*, not when you are about to *tune*. Dwork's Thresholdout only answers when the working set and the holdout disagree — so the operational rule is: run the held-out slice; if its pass rate agrees with the working set's within the noise floor (§4.5), you have learned that you did not overfit and you can keep going; if it is materially lower, you have learned that your working-set score is inflated, and the *next* action is to promote some held-out cases into the working set and mint fresh held-out cases — not to tune against the held-out ones. Every time you look and act on what you saw, that slice has become a little less held out. Count the looks.

**The refresh.** Two citable policies. LiveBench, built as a "contamination-limited" benchmark, rotates: "Each month, we do not release the new questions until one month later, so that the public leaderboard always has 1/6 questions that are private" — roughly a sixth of the set replaced per period, a sixth always unseen. GSM1k's approach is the rebuild: when a set is burned, generate a new one from the *same recipe* and compare. For a product set, no fetched source gives a numeric cadence; the Hamel–Shankar FAQ's review cycles run "2–4 weeks" with "10–20" traces reviewed weekly between them, and the OpenAI cookbook is explicit that "this is a continuous cycle. As you improve the system, new, subtler failure modes emerge — and the flywheel begins again." Tie your refresh to that cycle: every review cycle produces new golden cases (part 7); route a fixed fraction of them to the held-out slice before anyone tunes on them.

## 4.5 How big does the slice need to be? Error bars.

Evan Miller's "Adding Error Bars to Evals" (Anthropic, 2024) opens with the diagnosis: "Evals are commonly run and reported with a 'highest number is best' mentality … but not necessarily to test that result for any kind of statistical significance." "Evaluations are experiments." His recommendation is to report the standard error beside every mean, and the formula for a pass/fail eval is the Bernoulli one you derived in week 1:

```
SE = sqrt( p̂ (1 − p̂) / n )          95 % CI ≈ p̂ ± 1.96 · SE
```

At an observed pass rate of 80 %:

| n | normal approx. half-width | Wilson 95 % interval |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | ± 17.5 pp | 58.4 – 91.9 % |
| 40 | ± 12.4 pp | 65.2 – 89.5 % |
| 100 | ± 7.8 pp | 71.1 – 86.7 % |
| 400 | ± 3.9 pp | 75.8 – 83.6 % |
| 1,000 | ± 2.5 pp | 77.4 – 82.4 % |

The first column is the textbook formula, for intuition; the second is the Wilson score interval your `stats.py` will compute (it stays inside `[0, 1]` and is what the harness reports). Read the second row twice. A forty-case held-out slice that reports 80 % is telling you "somewhere between 65 % and 90 %." It cannot distinguish a five-point regression from noise. What it *can* do is catch a collapse — a drop from 80 % to 40 % is about four standard errors on the difference of two proportions — and confirm that the working set is not wildly inflated. That is its job at this size. Miller's power analysis gives the number for the job people *think* it does: to detect a 3-percentage-point difference at 80 % power, `n ≈ 969`, "rounded to a recommended 1,000 questions." Eugene Yan's practitioner version agrees: "If we run an experiment on 200 samples and observe 3 % defects, our 95 % confidence interval is roughly 3 % ± 2.4 %." (At small `n` or extreme `p̂` the normal approximation leaves `[0, 1]` — 40/40 gives exactly ±0 — which is why the harness uses Wilson. Your `metrics.py` from week 1 has the ingredients.)

Two of Miller's warnings apply directly to a harness built over documents or tools. **Clustering:** "When questions are drawn in related groups," compute clustered standard errors, because "failure to adjust standard errors for clustered sampling may lead an unsuspecting analyst to suppose that the measurement … is much more precise than it actually is" — on the DROP benchmark the clustered SE was 3.05× the naive one. If your forty cases are five questions over each of eight documents, your effective `n` is nearer eight than forty. **Pairing:** "Because eval question scores are likely to be positively correlated … paired differences represent a 'free' reduction in estimator variance when comparing two models." When you compare prompt A to prompt B, compare them *per case* — which cases flipped — not by subtracting two aggregate pass rates. Part 8 and the system-design drill build the gate on that.

## 4.6 Criteria drift: the leak that runs through you

There is a second contamination channel that has nothing to do with statistics. Shankar et al. observed, building a tool that helps people write evaluation criteria, "a phenomenon we dub criteria drift: users need criteria to grade outputs, but grading outputs helps users define criteria" — and, more pointedly, "some criteria appears dependent on the specific LLM outputs observed (rather than independent criteria that can be defined a priori)." Your definition of *correct* shifts as you read outputs. If you read the held-out slice's outputs while deciding what correct means, the slice's checks were written to match those outputs. Write the check before you run the case; when you must revise a check after seeing outputs, revise it on the working set and re-freeze the held-out slice's expectations only at the next refresh.

## 4.7 What to carry forward

Selecting prompts by their score on a case set fits the prompt to the set, and the score you read at the end is a training score with an optimistic bias you cannot measure from that set. The mechanism is proven (Blum–Hardt's dependence argument; Dwork's 50 %-reported-as-63 %), the endgame is visible in public benchmarks (GSM1k, SWE-bench Verified), and the steel-man (prompts overfit slowly) only lowers the rate. Keep a held-out slice: split before tuning; look at it to *ship*, not to *tune*; when you act on what you saw, refresh it and count the look. Size it honestly — forty cases gives ±12 points and catches collapses, not regressions; a thousand gives ±2.5 and detects a 3-point change. Compute clustered errors if your cases share sources, compare prompts per case rather than by aggregate, and write the check before you see the output.
